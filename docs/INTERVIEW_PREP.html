Truvel — Interview / Viva Prep

Interview & Viva Preparation

Built from docs/VIVA.md, SPEC.md and the project's own git history. Learn the bold line of each answer first — the rest is backup if they push.

30-second pitch: Truvel is a travel, social and creator app for India where people discover places to visit — including hidden ones that aren't on Google Maps — in their own language, and creators are rewarded mainly for the real visits they cause, not for likes or views. It runs on FastAPI + SQLite + React, uses only free/open data (OpenStreetMap, Wikidata, Wikimedia Commons), stores no real money and no user coordinates, and every ranking/learning/ fraud-detection algorithm in it is a small, tested, pure function evaluated by simulation.

Stack

FastAPI + SQLModel (SQLite+FTS5) · React 18 + TS · MapLibre/OpenFreeMap · zero paid services

Ranking

Wilson lower bound of likes/viewers — the cautious end of a 95% CI, not a raw ratio

Deck personalisation

Thompson sampling, Beta(α,β) per category, save→α+1, skip→β+1

Attribution

Time-decay: weight = 2^(−age_days / 7), normalised to sum to 1 per visit

Fraud

Rules (engaged-view definition) + spike rule: >4σ over 7-day hourly mean & >50 views

Duplicate places

<300m & ≥80% fuzzy name match (any language), or <100m & same category

Stolen photos

Perceptual hash (pHash), 64-bit fingerprint, reject within 8 bits Hamming distance

Verified visit

<150m at ≤100m GPS accuracy + impossible-travel check (>120 km/h)

Creator pool

Same 55% as YouTube Partner Program; split so 1 visit caused = 25 engaged views

Eligibility

30+ day account, no strikes, 10+ verified pieces, 300 visits or 100k views / 90 days

Privacy

No lat/lng columns anywhere; check-in keeps only distance + accuracy, discards coordinates

Data sources

Wikidata (CC0) · Commons photos (credited, allowlisted licences) · OSM (ODbL, 1 deep city)

A The idea

What is your project, in one sentence?
A travel, social and creator app for India where people discover places to visit, including hidden ones that aren't on Google Maps, in their own language, and creators are rewarded mainly for the visits they cause.
Isn't this just Google Maps, Instagram or YouTube?
Each feature exists somewhere; the combination doesn't. Google Local Guides rewards reviewing places already on Maps. YouTube/Instagram reward views, which favours entertainment over going outside. Tripoto/Explurger are trip-post social apps. Truvel has verified, unmapped places with last-mile directions and live conditions, and creator rewards tied to real visits, not engagement.
What makes people come back?
Things that pull them outside: the nearby deck, short videos with a "Go here" button, live conditions (Pulse), a fog map cleared by visiting, passport stamps, a weekly (not daily) streak, and kadam credit when someone visits a place they found. The deck deliberately ends — no infinite feed — and Watch nudges you after 20 minutes. The habit being built is going outside, not scrolling.
What's your moat? Couldn't Google copy you?
Google could copy any one feature; it's harder to copy the data and the incentives. Verified unmapped places, last-mile steps and Pulse get better with every visitor per city — a network effect. Paying for visits caused (not views) draws genuinely useful content. All scheduled Indian languages from day one, plus never storing location, build trust a general-purpose map app has no reason to offer.

B Money & the Partner Program

How do creators earn? Is it like YouTube?
Similar idea, different measure. YouTube shares 55% of ad revenue on long-form (45% on Shorts) with Partners. Truvel uses the same 55% pool figure, but splits it by a score where one visit caused counts as much as 25 engaged views, plus small amounts for helpful last-mile directions and confirmed live (Pulse) reports.
Does your app pay real money?
No. The pool is a simulated figure from config.yaml, and every ₹ on screen carries a "Simulated" label. Real payouts would need real revenue, identity/bank verification, TDS and a payment partner — that's roadmap; the engine and the maths are already built and tested, so turning it on is a business/compliance step, not an engineering one.
Where would the real money come from?
Local businesses near places (pay per verified visit sent their way), clearly labelled sponsored cards (at most 1 in 15), sponsored trails from tourism boards, and an optional Plus subscription. Rank itself is never for sale — a place never outranks another by paying.
Why not pay per like, like the original idea?
Likes are cheap to fake and reward looks, not usefulness. Paying per like invites bought likes, stolen photos and dangerous stunts for engagement. A visit means someone physically went there — much harder to fake, and it's the actual outcome the product exists for.
Who is eligible to earn?
Accounts 30+ days old, no strikes, 10+ pieces of verified content, and in the last 90 days either 300 visits caused or 100,000 engaged views. The thresholds exist specifically to stop fresh/fake accounts from farming small amounts.

C Data & languages

Where does your place data come from? Did you copy Google Maps?
No. Famous places across India come from Wikidata (CC0), photos from Wikimedia Commons (free licences, with attribution), and one city seeded deeply from OpenStreetMap (ODbL) — now extended to ten cities. Copying Google Maps would break its terms and the photographers' copyright; its Places API also forbids use with a non-Google map, limits how long coordinates can be cached, and is paid beyond a free tier. The app only uses the free "Open in Google Maps" link for directions, nothing more.
How do you get photos, and what licences are allowed?
From each Wikidata item's linked Commons image, fetched once and cached on the server, shown with author and licence on every photo. Only CC0, public domain, CC BY and CC BY-SA are accepted — anything else is hidden rather than shown without credit. Users add their own photos for hidden places, and the server strips EXIF from every upload.
How do you support India's many languages?
Place names come in all 22 scheduled languages + English from OSM's name:xx tags, Wikidata labels, and verified visitors adding a name in their own language. Each place shows the primary name plus the regional language's name in its own script. Fonts are self-hosted per script via @fontsource and only download when that script actually appears on screen.
Is the whole interface translated into 22 languages?
No — place names are; the UI is English first, then languages a native speaker has actually reviewed. A machine-translated UI presented as finished would be invariant 11 territory: it stays behind a "Beta" label until reviewed. A bad translation damages trust more than plain English does.
Why hide country/state borders on the map?
India has strict rules on how its external boundaries must be depicted, and free map data may not match the official depiction. The app doesn't need borders for anything it does, so those layers are hidden outright and state comes from Wikidata's administrative hierarchy instead of a boundary file.
What do you store about a user's location?
Nothing. At check-in the phone sends its position once; the server computes and keeps only the distance to the place and the GPS accuracy, then discards the coordinates. Visit, Pulse and Touch have no lat/lng columns at all — it's not just policy, the schema can't hold it.

D Algorithms (the data-science part)

How do you rank places? Why not sort by raw likes?
By the Wilson lower bound of likes ÷ viewers — the cautious end of a 95% confidence interval for the true like rate. Raw likes favour old places that have simply existed longer; a raw ratio favours 1 like out of 1 viewer. Worked example: 1/1 scores 0.21, 8/10 scores 0.49, 80/100 scores 0.71 — a place needs real evidence, not a lucky early visitor, to rank high.
app/ranking/wilson.py
Give me the Wilson formula.
With p̂ = k/n (likes/viewers) and z = 1.96: 
(p̂ + z²/2n − z·√(p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/4n²)) / (1 + z²/n)
How does the swipe deck learn what you like?
Thompson sampling — a multi-armed bandit. Each category keeps a Beta(α, β) belief, starting at Beta(1,1) (uniform prior, assumes nothing about a new user). Save → α+1, skip → β+1, open → no change (opening says "I wanted to look", not "I wanted to go"). Each deck draws one random sample per category from its belief and ranks by the draw, not the mean — a category with little data has a wide belief so its draw is sometimes high (exploration), costing nothing when it's genuinely bad since the draw is usually low too; a category saved many times has a narrow belief centred high and reliably wins (exploitation). Needs no training data and is fully explainable.
app/deck/thompson.py
What's the full deck scoring formula? 
score = 0.40·q + 0.25·prox + 0.25·pref + 0.10·fresh
q = percentile rank of the place's Wilson bound among current candidates · prox = exp(−distance_km / 4) (4km scores ~0.37, 1km ~0.78, 25km ~0.002) · pref = the Thompson draw for that category · fresh = 1 if added in the last 14 days or seen by fewer than 10 people, else 0. The weights say what the deck is for: mostly "is it good" and "can I get there", a quarter "is it my kind of thing", a tenth reserved so a good new find isn't invisible until it collects viewers.
How is the Watch feed ranked?
35% quality (Wilson of completions/engaged views) + 20% closeness + 20% taste (same Thompson sampling) + 15% freshness + 10% following, with a diversity re-rank capping any one creator at 2 videos in a row.
What counts as an "engaged view"?
At least 3 seconds watched (or half of a very short video); 90% watched counts as a completion. Views by the creator themself, by accounts under 24 hours old, or repeats by the same user within 24 hours, don't count toward anything.
How do you decide which creator "caused" a visit?
Time-decay attribution. On a person's first visit to a place, look at everything they engaged with about that place in the previous 30 days — the place page (credits the discoverer), videos, last-mile steps. Each touch gets weight 2^(−age_days / 7) — a video seen yesterday counts more than one seen 3 weeks ago. Weights are normalised so the visit's total credit sums to exactly 1, split across whoever touched it.
app/partner/attribution.py
Why time-decay and not simple last-touch or linear?
Because real-world decay is unknown, and time-decay is never far from best at any decay constant — the other two collapse at the opposite extreme. A sweep across ground-truth decay from 2 to 30 days: last-touch ranges 0.77–0.93 (great when recency is everything, poor otherwise), linear ranges 0.74–0.87 (the mirror case), time-decay stays 0.85–0.91 throughout. Since this rule divides real money between real people, robustness to being wrong about the world matters more than winning the best case. The half-life is a config value (attribution.half_life_days), tunable once real data exists.
How do you catch fake views?
Rules plus a spike detector. On top of the engaged-view rules above: if an item's views in one hour exceed its 7-day hourly mean by 4 standard deviations (and are over 50 in absolute terms), the excess is held out of earnings for review. Tier 2 adds an Isolation Forest (unsupervised anomaly detector) over daily creator features.
How do you detect duplicate places?
Distance plus fuzzy name matching, across every language the place has a name in. Within 300m and ≥80% name similarity to any candidate name (generic words like "lake"/"cheruvu"/"talab" stripped first), or within 100m and the same category. The app never silently merges — it asks "is it one of these?" and the person decides. (A stricter, automated variant with a plain character-ratio floor runs for bulk imports, since an unattended merge is worse than leaving a duplicate.)
app/integrity/dedupe.py
How do you catch stolen photos?
Perceptual hashing (pHash) — a 64-bit fingerprint of how an image looks, not its bytes. Copies that have been resized or recompressed stay within 8 bits (Hamming distance) of the original, so a near-identical re-upload is rejected. It can't catch a photo the app has never seen before — that's an accepted limitation, not a gap in the rule.
How do you know someone actually visited a place?
Geolocation at check-in: within 150m of the pin, at ≤100m GPS accuracy, plus an impossible-travel check (>120 km/h between two check-ins is flagged). GPS can be spoofed by a determined user — the honest claim is that this raises the cost of cheating, not that it makes cheating impossible.
What is Pulse?
Live conditions, postable only by people who checked in there in the last 2 hours: water flowing/dry, crowd level, path condition, open/closed. A report is "live" for 6 hours, "recent" for 48. If recent reports disagree, the app shows the majority and says "mixed reports" rather than picking one silently.

E Evaluation

How did you evaluate all this without real users?
Simulations with known ground truth, each over many random seeds with 95% confidence intervals, plus a real-data coverage table and the acceptance tests. Simulations prove the methods work under their stated assumptions — they don't prove real users behave that way. A user study is explicitly future work, not a swapped-in substitute.
sim/ranking_sim.pysim/bandit_sim.py sim/attribution_sim.pysim/fraud_sim.py
What did the simulations actually measure?
Ranking: Wilson vs raw-ratio precision@10. Bandit: Thompson save-rate vs popularity/random, early vs settled window. Attribution: time-decay vs last-touch vs linear Spearman correlation across decay constants. Fraud: precision/recall of the rule set and its effect on fraud's share of the simulated pool. See Section H below for how to defend the actual numbers under push-back.

F Engineering

What's the architecture?
React web app → FastAPI (Python) → SQLite, media on disk. Seed scripts pull from OpenStreetMap and Wikidata; the server proxies and caches Commons photos server-side so the frontend never calls a third party except map tiles. Maps use MapLibre with free OpenFreeMap tiles. It all runs on one laptop; a phone connects over HTTPS on the same Wi-Fi for testing.
Why a web app and not a native app?
Zero budget, one codebase, installable straight from the browser. A Play Store listing needs a paid developer account, which the project's hard constraints rule out.
How does the "fog of war" city map work?
A semi-transparent layer over the whole city with a ~1km hole punched around each verified visit (circles unioned and subtracted using Turf.js). Progress is counted in neighbourhoods: each visit belongs to the nearest neighbourhood point, equivalent to asking which Voronoi cell it falls in. The denominator only counts neighbourhoods that contain at least one real place — a neighbourhood with nowhere to go isn't somewhere the app can meaningfully send you (this was itself a discovered spec bug — see Section I).
What are the known limitations and next steps?
Simulations, not user studies; one (now ten) deeply-seeded cities out of thousands of Indian towns; GPS is spoofable by a determined attacker; money is simulated; the UI isn't yet translated beyond English + reviewed languages. Next, in priority order: a real outdoor user trip (unblocks several real-world measurements), a pHash robustness run against real photos instead of generated stand-ins, then CLIP embeddings — because the pHash result shows cropping defeats a plain hash completely, and CLIP cosine similarity is the documented Tier-2 answer to exactly that gap.

G Push-back questions

"Is this really AI, or just rules?"
It's applied data science inside a product, not a chatbot wrapper: ranking under uncertainty (Wilson score), online/reinforcement learning (Thompson bandit), attribution modelling (time-decay evaluated against two baselines), anomaly detection (a statistical spike rule now, Isolation Forest as Tier 2), similarity search (fuzzy name matching, perceptual hashing, CLIP as Tier 2), and neural transliteration (IndicXlit) — all evaluated by simulation with confidence intervals, not asserted to work.
"Isn't paying for visits gameable?"
A visit needs physical presence (GPS + impossible-travel check); attribution ignores self-touches, brand-new accounts and flagged visits; there are eligibility thresholds before anyone earns anything; a statistical spike rule holds suspicious bursts out of the pool; and removing bad content reverses the kadam/earnings it produced. It's harder to game than view-counting — not claimed to be impossible.
"What about dangerous or risky places?"
The safety card is never hidden or collapsible (invariant 9); fragile places are redacted (no exact pin, no last-mile steps) until a pledge; nothing in any ranking formula boosts risky content; emergency number 112 is always shown on a place page.
"Did you actually write this code, or did an AI?"
Built with Claude Code using spec-driven development: the spec and every phase plan were written and approved by the student, each phase was reviewed and tested before moving on, and every algorithm (wilson.py, thompson.py, attribution.py, split.py) can be explained line by line — which is the actual bar CLAUDE.md sets ("the student must explain every line in the viva").

H "Defend a number" — results walk-throughs

These are the exact traps an examiner sets: a metric that looks bad at first glance. Each answer below leads with why the headline metric is the wrong one to read, then gives the metric that actually matters.

Your simulation shows plain like-ratio beats Wilson on Kendall τ (0.621 vs 0.554). So why use Wilson?
Because τ scores the whole list, including places nobody will ever scroll to — and on precision@10, the only part a user reads, Wilson wins: 0.452 vs 0.356. Wilson deliberately shrinks a place seen by six people toward zero, because six people isn't evidence; that shrinkage misorders the long tail (hurting τ) but is exactly what stops 2-likes-of-2 beating 1,600-of-2,000 at the top. Mean true like-rate of the top 10 is 0.651 under Wilson vs 0.592 under raw ratio — reporting both numbers rather than only the flattering one is itself part of the answer.
Your bandit sim shows Thompson sampling is 8.4% worse than popularity over the first 30 swipes. Why ship it?
Because the same simulation shows it's 7.0% better once it's learned — and the deck is built for people who come back, not a one-visit kiosk. Over the first 30 swipes Thompson is still drawing from a uniform prior across 8 categories, i.e. paying to learn what you like (~1 save in 30). By the settled window (last 30 of 150) it beats popularity 0.155 to 0.145. That crossover is the exploration/exploitation trade-off, stated honestly rather than hidden. (Next step if asked: seed the prior from population-average taste instead of uniform — the sim's own "fixed weights" baseline already shows what that looks like.)
Your fraud rules have recall of 0.786 — a quarter of fraud gets through. Isn't that a failure?
Recall is the wrong headline; precision is 1.000, and fraud's share of the pool drops from 0.598 to 0.278 — that's the number that matters. Precision 1.000 over 50 runs means the rules never once flagged an honest view (a false positive here takes real money from a real creator — worse than letting a bot view through). The rules remove 53.6% of what fraud would have earned. Crucially the sim scores views only, to isolate the view rules — the real defence is that a visit is worth 25 views in partner.weights, and a visit needs a person physically present with GPS accuracy under 100m, no impossible travel, and two independent confirmations before a brand-new place counts at all. Honest conclusion: these rules raise the cost of fraud, they don't end it. A patient attacker with aged accounts, one view each, at human pace, would evade all four rules.
Why is time-decay "right" when your own sweep shows last-touch wins at 2-day decay and linear wins at 30?
Because nobody knows the true decay constant, and time-decay is the only one of the three that's never far from best at any of them. See the full answer in Section D. The short version for a quick follow-up: picking last-touch or linear is a bet on a parameter that hasn't been measured; time-decay costs a little at both extremes and never collapses, which is the right trade when the thing being decided divides real money between real people.

I Bugs found in the spec or code, and how

What bugs did you find in your own spec, and how?

1. Four Wikidata class QIDs were wrong. SPEC says "verify each QID in the query service" — doing that showed Q842425 is a chef, not a gurdwara; Q1066288 is "figurine", not a stepwell; Q2082441 is a record producer, not a ghat. Skipping the check would have silently returned zero results for those classes.

2. The kadam daily caps were ambiguous — "pulse +3, 5 per day" reads as five events a day, not five kadam. Implemented as event counts and documented in kadam.py.

3. The deck's diversity re-rank had a real bug — the obvious greedy ("best card that doesn't extend the current run") strands a long run at the very end of the list. Found by writing a test that stressed the tail, not the head. Fixed with a closed-form check: largest_group <= k * (total - largest_group + 1).

4. The fog-map denominator didn't survive real data — real OSM data gives ~1,400 neighbourhood-tagged nodes for one city, mostly residential colonies with nothing to visit, so the honest count read "6 of 1,408 (0%)". Changed the denominator to neighbourhoods containing at least one real place (244 for Hyderabad) — flagged explicitly as a spec change needing sign-off, not silently patched in.

What real security/privacy bugs did a focused review find later?

Fragile-place coordinates were recoverable without ever pledging. The API correctly rounded the returned lat/lng, but computed the distance field from the viewer's point to the place's true, unrounded coordinates. Three distance readings from three known points is enough to trilaterate the exact pin. One-line fix: derive distance from the already-fuzzed coordinates, never from the row underneath them — and the same bug existed independently in the duplicate-candidates endpoint.

A kadam/earnings shortcut existed for video/photo "touches". Real engaged-view touches are already created server-side the moment a watch session is classified as engaged — but a separate code path let a client claim a video/photo touch directly with no verification at all. Fixed by deleting the unverifiable path, since the verified one already existed elsewhere (two ways to create the same row is itself the vulnerability, because only one of them was ever checked).

A genuine check-in race condition — two simultaneous requests (two tabs, a retry, a scripted double-call) could both pass a "haven't checked in today" read before either commit landed, double-paying kadam. Fixed with a lock keyed by (user_id, place_id) around the whole read-then-write section — reasonable given the whole app runs as one process on one laptop. A 5-thread concurrent test proves exactly one ledger entry results.
